Mechanics

How a matching engine works

An order book is two sorted collections of resting limit orders — bids below, asks above — and the gap between the best of each is the spread. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Price-time priority

The rule almost every equity venue uses to decide who fills first:

  • Price priority — better prices execute first. A 100.50 bid always fills before a 100.45 bid, regardless of who arrived first.
  • Time priority — among orders at the same price, first in fills first.

Time priority at a price level is why a deque per level is the natural structure: append to the back on arrival, pop from the front when filling. The queue is the priority.

Which is why queue position is a tradable asset. If you're 5,000 shares deep at the best bid and only 3,000 trade there before the price moves, you don't get filled at all. Market makers spend real effort getting to the front and staying there — which is also why most cancels in real markets aren't changes of mind, they're repositioning.

Three details worth getting right

Trades print at the maker's price. A resting sell at 101.00 meets an incoming buy willing to pay 101.50 — the trade prints at 101.00 and the buyer gets price improvement. The whole maker/taker economic model rests on this. Get it backwards and you've inverted the incentive structure of the entire venue.

A market order is a limit order without the price condition. Both go through one _match() loop; the only difference is whether a price ceiling is checked. Keeping matching in exactly one place is what stops the two paths from silently disagreeing.

Maker or taker is a property of the fill, not the order. A 500-share buy that clears a 300-share ask took liquidity for 300 and then became the passive side with the remaining 200.

Emergent results

Four things nobody programmed in

1. A spread distribution

The spread is an equilibrium between two forces the simulator does specify: market orders eat depth at the touch, passive limit orders replace it. When consumption outruns replacement the touch empties and the spread widens; when replacement wins, someone posts inside and it collapses back to one tick. If you'd only ever seen this output you might reach for an explanation involving market makers managing inventory and adverse selection. There are no market makers here. There is no inventory. There is no information.

2. A hump-shaped depth profile

This is the least realistic output here, and the reason is instructive. Zero-intelligence agents never reprice. A real trader who posts three ticks away and watches the market leave pulls the order and reposts; here it sits until a random cancel happens to select it. The shape is emergent; the magnitude is an artifact. Distinguishing those two is the entire skill in reading a simulation.

3. Concave price impact

Bigger orders move the price more — obviously — but sublinearly: impact scales as size—. Doubling the order size costs less than double the impact. That concavity is one of the most robust empirical facts in market microstructure, and here it emerges from nothing but walking a book with a hump-shaped depth profile. Real markets sit closer to square-root (exponent ≈ 0.5); the gap is the depth profile again.

4. A mid that is not a random walk

A random walk's variance grows linearly with the horizon — double the time, double the variance. This doesn't: at k=128 the variance is only ~22× the one-step variance, not 128×. Fitted scaling gives a Hurst exponent of — against 0.50 for a true random walk, with lag-1 autocorrelation of —. The mid mean-reverts at short horizons.

The most useful result here is a warning. Real tick data shows this same signature, and a naive reading says "the mid mean-reverts, therefore buy dips at the tick level." But you cannot trade the mid — you buy at the ask and sell at the bid. The mean reversion here is about 0.001 per event against a mean spread of 0.0198. The signal is roughly twenty times smaller than the cost of acting on it.

Two mechanisms produce it, both purely mechanical: bid-ask bounce (the mid ticks up when the best ask is consumed and down when the best bid is, alternating roughly at random) and the restoring force of resting depth (a market buy that lifts the touch leaves a thinner ask and a fatter bid behind it). If your alpha model finds mean reversion at the tick level, you have probably rediscovered bid-ask bounce. Test against a zero-intelligence null before believing otherwise.

Performance

The comment that said "fine at this scale"

Best price used to be max(bids) / min(asks) over the price dictionary, with a comment saying a sorted structure was "a profiling-driven optimization, not where you start." That's a reasonable place to start and a bad place to stay: the lookup is read on every quote, every pass of the matching loop and every event the simulator generates, and it's the one operation in the book whose cost grows with depth.

Same 20,000-event run, seeded to different depths. Two implementations timed in the same process — the control overrides one method and shares everything else, so the gap is best-price lookup and nothing else.

LevelsScan lookupHeap lookupScanHeap

Each side now keeps a binary heap of its live price levels, so the best price is a peek instead of a scan. Deletion is lazy — emptying a level doesn't touch the heap, and the stale entry is discarded when it surfaces at the top — because removing an arbitrary element from a heap is O(L) and would hand back exactly what was just won.

A differential test runs the heap and the old scan side by side over random order flow and asserts they agree after every single event. An optimization that changes an answer isn't one.

What the benchmark found next: cancel had the fastest median and the worst tail. Per-operation latency at 500 levels, in microseconds — —. That was deque.remove(), which is O(orders at that level): cancelling at the front of a queue is instant, cancelling at the back walks the whole thing, and the caller can neither see nor choose which one they are. In real equity markets well over 90% of orders are cancelled rather than filled, so that was the hot path wearing the worst distribution.

Same fix, one layer down: don't delete, tombstone

cancel() now flags the order inactive, drops its id, decrements the level's live-order count and returns. The order stays physically in its queue until the matching loop reaches it and throws it away — the one moment the work was unavoidable anyway. Per-level live counts are what keep it honest: when the last live order at a price goes, the price key is deleted in O(1), so the quotes, the depth and the heaps never see a level nobody is quoting, and the dropped queue takes its tombstones with it.

It also flattened the throughput curve above. The heaps left a 2.2× fall-off from 10 to 1,000 levels; cancel was what remained of it, and the sweep is now flat end to end.

Limitations

What isn't modeled

  • Single symbol, single venue. No routing, no NBBO, no Reg NMS.
  • Order types stop at limit, market, IOC and FOK — no stops, icebergs, pegged or auction-only orders.
  • No opening or closing auction, where a large share of real daily volume actually trades, under entirely different rules.
  • No latency. Every order arrives instantly and in submission order, which erases the entire subject matter of low-latency trading.
  • Self-trade prevention exists (participant IDs, two cancellation policies); fees, rebates and other risk checks don't.
  • Agents have no memory, inventory or information. That is what makes it a valid null model, and what makes the depth magnitudes wrong.
